## What this page covers

A dwelling can remain physically similar while its legal or practical use changes significantly. Issues can arise where a house is divided internally, occupied on a room-by-room basis, converted into separate units, intensively occupied, or otherwise used differently from the use previously established.

The correct legal description depends on the facts. Relevant concepts can include **subdivision**, **material alteration**, **material change of use**, **multiple occupation**, **separate residential units**, and **room-by-room letting**.

## Planning law comes first

A change in how a property is used may require planning permission even where little visible building work takes place. Conversely, not every internal rearrangement or room-by-room letting arrangement automatically amounts to a material change of use.

The key question is whether, as a matter of fact and degree, the character of the use has changed in a way that is material for planning purposes.

## Physical subdivision

Creating separate self-contained residential units can involve works such as:

- new kitchens or kitchenettes
- additional bathrooms
- internal partitions or locked divisions
- separate entrances
- fire separation works
- separate utility arrangements

Those works may engage planning, building-control and fire-safety requirements in addition to tenancy law.

## Room-by-room letting

Letting individual bedrooms in one house does not necessarily mean the property has been converted into separate dwelling units. The factual arrangement matters, including whether occupants share kitchens, bathrooms and common areas, whether each occupier has an individual agreement, and whether the house continues to function as one dwelling.

## Material change of use

Irish planning law recognises the concept of a **material change in use**. Whether a particular change is material is highly fact-specific and may depend on planning history, intensity of occupation, physical configuration, impact on the surrounding area, and the precise nature of the new use.

The Planning and Development Acts should be checked together with current local-authority guidance and any planning history for the property.

## Short-term letting is a distinct category

Legislation in force in 2026 expressly addresses short-term letting as a material change in use in specified circumstances. That does not mean every form of ordinary residential room letting is automatically treated the same way.

## Other compliance questions

A change in occupation or configuration can also raise questions about:

- minimum standards for rented accommodation
- fire safety and escape routes
- overcrowding
- building regulations
- insurance conditions
- registration of tenancies
- whether occupants are tenants or licensees
- local authority enforcement

These regimes overlap but are legally distinct. Compliance with one does not automatically establish compliance with the others.

## Before altering or reletting

A prudent owner should establish the planning status of the property before carrying out works or materially changing its use. Where the position is uncertain, planning records, the local authority and professional planning or legal advice may be needed.

## Evidence and planning history

Useful records can include:

- planning permissions and planning-register entries
- approved drawings
- building-control records
- fire-safety documentation
- photographs of the configuration before and after works
- advertisements describing the intended use
- tenancy or licence agreements
- utility arrangements
